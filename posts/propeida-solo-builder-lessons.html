What building Propeida solo taught me about product decisions

The problem: too many features, nothing shipped

When I started building Propeida, I believed the hardest part would be writing the code. I was wrong: the hardest part was deciding what not to build. Every feature I imagined felt essential in my head, but building alone meant every yes cost me hours I did not have. I had to learn that product decisions are not about what you can build, but about what you should build, and more importantly, what you should refuse to build.

Propeida is an exam preparation platform for Nigerian students. It offers practice questions, leaderboards, and admin tools for publishing content. In my first plan, I listed fourteen features. I thought I needed all of them to make the product complete. Two weeks in, I had built nothing users could touch. I was stuck in planning mode, adding more features to the list instead of shipping the ones that mattered.

The three questions that changed everything

The turning point came when I started asking three questions before every feature. The first question was: does this directly help a student prepare for an exam? The second question was: can I ship a basic version of this in a single day? The third question was: will I still want to maintain this six months from now? These three questions changed how I thought about building. They forced me to separate what felt important from what actually was.

The referral tracking feature almost made it into the first launch: it felt like a growth lever, with students sharing the platform with friends, tracking referrals, and unlocking rewards. It sounded good on paper, but when I looked at it honestly, it added enormous surface area: I would need share links, a tracking page, reconciliation logic, and a rewards system. None of that helped a student practice for an exam, so I cut it; the platform launched without it, and nobody asked for it.

What I cut and why it worked

The leaderboard taught me another lesson: I wanted it to be perfect: real-time updates, tie-breaking rules, weekly resets, and historical rankings. I spent three days designing the logic before I wrote a single line of code. When I finally shipped a simple version, just a SQL query that ranked users by score, it worked fine, and students used it every day. The fancy features I had imagined would have added complexity without adding value.

I also learned that every feature is a bet against your own future time. When you build a feature, you are not just writing code once: you are committing to maintaining it, debugging it, and explaining it to users forever. A solo builder cannot afford that commitment lightly. Every feature I add to Propeida is a weight I carry. Some weights are worth it, such as the practice loop and the leaderboard. The referral system would have been a weight I carried for no reason.

The hardest product decision I made was cutting the admin dashboard: I wanted a full admin panel where teachers could upload questions, manage users, and view analytics, and it was the feature I was most excited about. But building it properly would have taken weeks, so I shipped without it and managed questions directly in the database, which was ugly but functional. Teachers could email me questions and I would add them manually; it was not scalable, but it was honest. The platform worked, students used it, and I learned what they actually needed before I built the admin tools.

The real lesson about constraints

Building solo also taught me that product decisions are not just about features: they are about focus. When you are alone, you cannot do everything; you have to choose one thing and do it well. Propeida chose to be the best practice platform for Nigerian entrance exams: it did not try to be a social network, a tutoring service, or a marketplace, and that focus made every decision easier.

The biggest lesson I took from building Propeida is that constraints are not limitations: they are design tools. My limited time forced me to make decisions I might have avoided if I had more resources; I could not afford to build everything, so I built only what mattered. The result was a product that was smaller than I originally imagined, but sharper: it did one thing well instead of fourteen things poorly.

Frequently asked questions
How many users did Propeida have at launch?

Propeida served 250+ active users in its first week of launch, with 1,200+ practice attempts in the first three weeks.

What tech stack does Propeida use?

Next.js for the frontend, Supabase for auth and database, Paystack for payments, and Tailwind CSS for styling.

Why did you cut the referral tracking feature?

It added significant surface area (share links, tracking page, reconciliation logic) without directly helping students practice for exams. The core loop was practice, not referrals.

What would you do differently if you started over?

I would cut even faster. The first version should have been just questions and a leaderboard. Everything else could have waited for real user feedback.
