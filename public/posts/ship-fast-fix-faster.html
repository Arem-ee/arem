Ship fast, fix faster: my approach to building as a student

Oct 2026 5 min read
TL;DR

Ten fast iterations teach you more than one long month of building alone. Shipping is a skill, not just about writing code.

In this post
  • What ship fast means
  • What fix faster means
  • Why spending all day helps and hurts

What ship fast means

Ship fast does not mean shipping bad work: it means shipping the smallest version of your idea that still helps people. When I launched Propeida, the look was plain: no animations, no fancy design, and no admin panel, but it worked: students could practice questions, see their scores, and compare with others, and that was enough.

The mistake most builders make is waiting until everything is perfect before showing anyone. They spend weeks making things look nice that users might not even want. By the time they launch, they are tired and the project dies. I learned to launch when the product is good enough, not when it is perfect. Good enough means it solves a real problem for real people. Perfect means it solves the problem with no flaws at all. Only one of those is worth sending out.

Ship fast also means cutting features without mercy. Every feature I add is a promise. I have to keep it working, fix it when it breaks, and explain it to users forever. When you spend all day on a project, it feels like you can build everything, but you cannot: every feature you add takes time away from the ones that already exist. The referral tracking system I cut from Propeida would have been fun to build, but it would not have helped students practice. so cutting it was not a loss; it was the right choice.

What fix faster means

Fix faster means treating every bug as a lesson. When users reported that leaderboard scores were not updating, I did not just fix the bug. I added logging so the next problem would be easy to find. When the practice flow felt confusing, I watched someone use the platform and rewrote the confusing part the same day. Every fix makes the product better, but every fix also teaches you how to avoid the next one.

The rhythm is simple: send out something small, watch how people use it, fix what breaks or confuses, and do it again; this cycle works much better than spending weeks building in secret and hoping everything works when you finally show it.

Why spending all day helps and hurts

Spending all day on projects sounds like a big advantage, and it is, but it has risks. The main risk is losing sight of what matters. When you are deep in the code, every feature feels important. You lose the ability to see what users actually care about. I had to learn to step back and ask: does this help the user, or does it just feel like progress?

The advantage is speed: when you have time, you can ship a feature, get feedback, fix it, and ship again all in one day, and that cycle is where real learning happens. Each pass teaches you something the last one missed. The students who build the most are not always the ones with the most talent; they are the ones who go through the most cycles.

The real lesson

The real lesson is that shipping is a skill: it is not just about writing code, but about making choices, cutting scope, and accepting that the first version will not be perfect; it is about trusting that you will make it better after real users start using it.

Spending all day on projects taught me that hours do not matter as much as cycles. Ten fast iterations teach you more than one long month of building alone. Ship fast, fix faster, and let the rhythm of building and learning carry you further than hard work alone ever could.

Frequently asked questions
How do you decide when something is ready to ship?

When it solves a real problem for real users, even if the design is rough or some features are missing. Good enough beats perfect every time.

What is the biggest mistake student builders make?

Waiting too long to launch. They spend weeks polishing features nobody asked for instead of shipping something small and learning from real users.

How do you avoid burnout when spending all day on projects?

By shipping small wins often. Each iteration gives you feedback and a sense of progress, which keeps the work sustainable over time.

Should you always cut features from your first version?

Yes, especially as a solo builder. Cut anything that does not directly serve the core value of the product. You can always add it later based on user feedback.
